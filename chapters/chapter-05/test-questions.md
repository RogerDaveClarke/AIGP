# Chapter 5: Test Questions - Comparative Approaches to AI Regulation

> Practice questions in the style of the AIGP certification exam (single-best-answer multiple choice, 4 options). Try to answer all questions before checking the answer key at the bottom.

## Questions

1. Which best describes a "risk-based" approach to AI regulation, as exemplified by the EU AI Act?
   A. All AI systems are regulated identically regardless of use case
   B. Obligations scale with the level of risk an AI system poses (e.g., unacceptable, high, limited, minimal)
   C. Only AI systems that fail are regulated
   D. Regulation applies only to generative AI

2. Which jurisdiction has pursued a largely voluntary, "pro-innovation" sectoral approach to AI regulation rather than a single comprehensive AI law?
   A. European Union
   B. United Kingdom
   C. South Korea
   D. Brazil

3. What is a defining feature of Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework?
   A. It is a binding statute with criminal penalties
   B. It is a voluntary, practical framework to help organizations operationalize responsible AI
   C. It only applies to government agencies
   D. It bans generative AI outright

4. Which of the following best characterizes China's approach to AI regulation?
   A. A single, unified comprehensive AI law predating all others
   B. A series of targeted regulations (e.g., on algorithmic recommendations, deep synthesis, and generative AI services) alongside broader strategic plans
   C. A complete absence of any AI-specific rules
   D. Reliance solely on international treaties

5. Why do comparative AI regulation surveys matter for multinational organizations?
   A. Because only one jurisdiction's rules ever apply globally
   B. Because organizations operating across borders must navigate varying and sometimes conflicting regulatory requirements
   C. Because AI regulation is identical everywhere
   D. Because comparative analysis is irrelevant to compliance planning

6. Which of the following is an example of a "soft law" instrument in AI governance, as opposed to binding legislation?
   A. The EU AI Act
   B. OECD AI Principles
   C. GDPR
   D. The AI Basic Act (South Korea)

7. India's national AI strategy and governance guidelines are primarily characterized by:
   A. A single binding comprehensive AI statute enacted before 2020
   B. A mix of strategic plans (e.g., National Strategy for AI) and sector-specific guidance rather than one omnibus law
   C. A total ban on AI research
   D. Full adoption of the EU AI Act text

8. What is a common rationale given for countries adopting principles-based rather than prescriptive, rules-based AI regulation?
   A. Principles-based regulation guarantees zero compliance burden
   B. Flexibility to adapt to rapidly evolving technology without constant legislative amendment
   C. It removes the need for any enforcement mechanism
   D. It is required under international law

9. Which factor most often differentiates "comprehensive" AI laws (like the EU AI Act) from "sectoral" regulatory approaches (like parts of the U.S. approach)?
   A. Comprehensive laws regulate AI horizontally across sectors and risk tiers, while sectoral approaches regulate AI through existing sector-specific regulators/laws
   B. Sectoral approaches always preempt comprehensive laws
   C. There is no meaningful difference
   D. Comprehensive laws exempt high-risk systems

10. Why might a country rely on existing general-purpose laws (e.g., consumer protection, anti-discrimination) rather than passing new AI-specific legislation?
    A. Existing laws are believed to already cover many AI-related harms, reducing urgency for new AI-specific statutes
    B. AI is legally undefined everywhere
    C. It is impossible to apply old laws to new technology
    D. International law prohibits new AI legislation

## Answers

1. B
2. B
3. B
4. B
5. B
6. B
7. B
8. B
9. A
10. A
