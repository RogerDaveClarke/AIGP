## Summary
### Ecosystem: Typical actors
- Standards bodies who provide guidance on how to implement AI governance
- Research institutes publish technical and policy research advancing responsible and human centric AI. This research comes from universities, governments and non-profits
- Vendors supply platforms and services to support AI governance
- Assurance providers who audit AI systems to valid they are operating as expected and comply with legal regulations
- Professional associations provide opportunties to learn, grown and join AI practioneers communities
- Customers procure and use AI systems with an increasing demand for transparency in how they are governed


### AI Learning and Development
Organizations are approach this based on three core themes:
- Foundational understand of AI
- Advanced knowledge (risk mitigation, compliance or ethics)
- Domain knowledge is an understanding of specific functions

**Recommendations**
- Integrate governance into employee onboarding and company training programs
- Use multiple formats with real-world examples
- Integrate critical thinking skills
- Use a central repositor, curate and refresh content
- Utilise metrics to determine engagement
  
There are 8 recognized functions with AI governance, akin to the European Cyber Security Skills Framework.
- Policy: Aligns organizational policies with the AI policy landscape, creates internal/external policies for development, deployment and use of AI systems and training and awareness for these. The skills needed are Researches and analyse laws, regulations and industry standards, draft policies, engage and align stakeholders. Key knowledge areas are: Organizational strategy for AI and its governance, AI development life cycle, principles and concepts, relevant laws,  regulations and industry standards 
- Governance: Create framework policies and controls. Key skills are Build/procure tools to run the program, track and report metrics and risks, monitor compliance and promote literacy and upskilling
